# Phase 8 — Distributed systems and reliable integration

**Goal:** Reason about the assumptions, guarantees, and failure modes that arise when independent processes communicate over networks and maintain data on separate machines. Then apply those fundamentals to messages, providers, and distributed workflows.

**Implementation context:** Reuse the P4.7b fault-injection provider and P4.8a contract sheet. Use one clearly labelled synthetic booking/PMS integration as the cumulative case. A lab is not a production incident. Select one broker for the runnable implementation; use the other as a design comparison unless the project requires both.

## 1. Foundation before patterns

- **P8.0a — System models, networks, and failure detection** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P3.5–P3.6, P4.1. Model processes, links, timing assumptions, synchronous versus asynchronous communication, crash-stop versus crash-recovery behavior, delay, loss, duplication, reordering, and network partitions. Understand why a timeout gives suspicion, not proof that a remote process crashed. Introduce flow control and congestion control as network behavior, distinct from application-level backpressure in P9.6/P9.7. **Proof:** failure timeline for lost, delayed, and duplicated request/response messages; label what each participant knows and what remains unknown. Sources: S27 ch. 2–7 and 23 (selected sections read); S26 ch. 4 (read); F4.
- **P8.0b — Distributed time, causality, and ordering** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P6.5, P8.0a. Distinguish wall clocks, monotonic clocks, clock skew, clock drift, physical timestamps, logical clocks, Lamport ordering, vector clocks, and happens-before causality. Do not assume timestamps establish a total order across machines. **Proof:** analyze events from multiple nodes with skew and concurrent execution; state which order is known, unknown, or business-defined. Sources: S19 ch. 9 (read: distributed time); S27 ch. 8 (selected sections read).
- **P8.0c — Replication and consistency models** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P6.5, P8.0a–P8.0b. Compare single-leader, multi-leader, and leaderless replication; synchronous/asynchronous replication; stale reads; read-your-writes and monotonic reads; eventual, causal, sequential, and linearizable consistency; quorums and conflict resolution. Explain what CAP does and does not say under a partition. Treat CRDTs as a recognition/design option here; deep CRDT implementation is specialist work. **Proof:** choose consistency requirements for three access paths and model a stale read or concurrent conflicting write, including the user-visible consequence. Sources: S19 ch. 6 and 10 (selected sections read); S27 ch. 10–11 (selected sections read); S76, PostgreSQL 18 docs, ch. 26.
- **P8.0d — Partitioning, sharding, and rebalancing** — **Priority:** important. Depends on: P5.4, P5.8, P8.0c. Distinguish table partitioning in one database from distributing shards across nodes. Compare range/hash partitioning, consistent hashing, partition keys, hot partitions/tenants, routing, rebalancing, and locality. **Proof:** choose a partition key for a stated workload, simulate skew, and document the rebalancing and query consequences. Sources: S19 ch. 7 (read); S28 ch. 10–12 [TOC]; S29 ch. 7 (selected sections read); S76 §5.12 for PostgreSQL table partitioning.
- **P8.0e — Consensus, leader election, leases, and coordination** — **Priority:** important. Depends on: P6.3, P8.0a, P8.0c. Explain consensus at the level needed to reason about agreement, leader election, safety, liveness, quorum, and failure recovery. Understand why distributed locks need lease/fencing reasoning and why an expired lease alone cannot stop an old paused process from writing. Raft implementation and consensus internals are optional specializations, not a default coding requirement. **Proof:** analyze a stale-leader timeline and show how a monotonically increasing fencing token rejects a zombie write; explain when coordination can be avoided. Sources: S19 ch. 9–10 (selected sections read); S27 ch. 9–11 (selected sections read); S18 course pointer only.

## 2. Messaging and distributed workflows

- **P8.1 — Partial failure, ambiguous outcomes, and delivery semantics** — **Priority:** important. Depends on: P4.5, P6.1–P6.5, P8.0a–P8.0b. A timeout does not prove that a remote side effect did not occur. Distinguish message delivery from business-effect execution. Introduce at-most-once, at-least-once, and exactly-once as boundary-specific claims, not end-to-end promises. **Proof:** failure timeline for a request that times out after the remote write; mark what each participant knows and the guarantee at each boundary. Sources: S26 ch. 4, S29 ch. 2 (read), S64–S66, S19 ch. 9.
- **P8.2 — Idempotency and deduplication** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P6.5, P8.1. Define the logical operation key, duplicate scope, retention, and response behavior. For database-backed consumers, record the message/operation key and apply the corresponding state change in the same database transaction. A local inbox row does not make an external side effect atomic; use provider idempotency where supported and reconciliation where the outcome remains ambiguous. **Proof:** repeat one operation sequentially and concurrently and show that it cannot create a second business effect; test a crash boundary. Sources: S29 ch. 2, S64–S65, S19 ch. 8 [TOC].
- **P8.3 — Retry budgets, deadlines, backoff, and jitter** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P4.5, P8.1–P8.2. P4.5 owns provider-specific HTTP outcome classification. This unit owns maximum attempts, elapsed-time budgets, deadlines, capped exponential backoff, jitter, retry amplification, and the rule to retry side-effecting operations only when idempotency is understood. Honor a provider-supplied retry delay when its contract requires it; avoid infinite or immediate retry loops. **Proof:** bounded policy tested against 429/Retry-After, transient 5xx, timeout-after-write, and permanent failure; show attempt count and elapsed time stay within budget. Sources: S25, F10, S29 ch. 16 (read), S66.
- **P8.4 — Webhook receiver and sender contracts** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P4.2–P4.6, P4.8a, P6.5, P8.1–P8.3. Verify the provider's signature over the required bytes, validate event type/payload, apply replay and duplicate protection, persist receipt, enqueue durable work, and acknowledge according to that provider's contract. Duplicate and out-of-order delivery are design cases. Never copy one provider's headers, signature rules, timeout, or retry policy to another. **Proof:** local tests for invalid signature, replay, duplicate, out-of-order delivery, transient processing failure, and crash windows. **Sources:** S67–S68 (provider-specific official contracts), F11, S26 ch. 4.
- **P8.5 — Queues, workers, acknowledgements, poison messages, and DLQ** — Implementation unit (multi-session). **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P3.5–P3.6, P5.6, P8.2–P8.4. First model the vendor-neutral lifecycle: publish, receive, process, persist effect, acknowledge/commit, crash, redelivery, and terminal failure. Then compare broker-specific semantics. Kafka: partitions, per-partition order, consumer groups, offsets, commits, retained-log replay, and application-owned retry/dead-letter topics. RabbitMQ: manual acknowledgements, reject/nack/requeue, publisher confirms, prefetch, dead-letter exchanges, and quorum-queue delivery limits. Do not equate a Kafka offset with a RabbitMQ acknowledgement or publisher confirms with consumer acknowledgements. Poison-message handling must bound redelivery, preserve payload/diagnostic metadata, alert an operator, and define audited repair/replay. DLQ is an operational workflow, not a place to forget messages. RabbitMQ quorum-queue at-least-once dead lettering is opt-in and configuration-constrained; do not generalize it to every queue type. **Proof:** worker restart without duplicate business effects; bounded transient retry; poison message reaches a terminal route; healthy work continues; required per-entity ordering is tested. Sources: S19 ch. 12 (read), S26 ch. 4–5 and 12 (selected sections read), S72 [VERIFY before teaching], S73 (reviewed sections).
- **P8.6 — Dual writes, transactional outbox, saga, and compensation** — Implementation unit (multi-session). **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P6.4, P8.1–P8.5. Separate the database/broker dual-write problem from a multi-service business workflow. The outbox stores a business change and event record in one local transaction; a relay may publish more than once, so consumers still need idempotency. Contrast two-phase commit/atomic commit with saga-based coordination, including coordinator/recovery and availability trade-offs. A saga coordinates local transactions; compensation is a new business action, not rollback of history. Model the saga as an explicit state machine. Distinguish orchestration from choreography and define timeout, retry, and terminal-state behavior for every step. **Proof:** implement a transactional outbox in Python/PostgreSQL; crash the relay between database commit and publish, restart it, and show eventual publication plus consumer deduplication. Model one multi-step booking saga and document compensations and their limits. Sources: S19 ch. 8 (selected sections read), ch. 13 [TOC]; S26 ch. 6 and 12; S38 ch. 11–12 (selected sections read).
- **P8.7 — Synchronization, event replay, and reconciliation** — **Priority:** must-have. Depends on: P4.8a, P6.5, P8.2–P8.6. Define source of truth, full/delta sync, cursors/watermarks, drift categories, replay scope, and repair rules. Distinguish replaying a retained Kafka log via offsets from redriving RabbitMQ dead-lettered/archived messages; these are not interchangeable and both require duplicate-safe processing. State retention limits and eligible event sets. **Proof:** reconciliation report classifying missing, duplicate, stale, and conflicting records, plus a replay/repair test that fixes a seeded discrepancy without a second business effect. Sources: S19 ch. 12 (read), S26, S27 ch. 10–11 (selected sections read), S72 [VERIFY before teaching], S73.
- **P8.8 — CQRS and event sourcing** — **Priority:** useful. Depends on: P6.6, P7.9, P8.0b, P8.2. Distinguish commands from queries, write models from read projections, domain events from messages, and an append-only event history from a conventional current-state table. Cover projection rebuilding, schema evolution, event versioning, and the operational cost of retaining/replaying history. **Proof:** design a small event stream and read projection for one workflow, compare it with a simpler CRUD design, and record when CQRS/event sourcing is not justified. Sources: S19 ch. 3 and 5 (selected sections read); S20 ch. 15 and 20 [TOC]; S38 ch. 9 [TOC].
- **P8.9 — Change data capture and database-to-stream integration** — **Priority:** useful. Depends on: P5.9, P8.5–P8.6. Understand log-based CDC versus polling/dual-write approaches, snapshot plus change stream, ordering/positions, schema changes, tombstones, and downstream consumer recovery. **Proof:** choose a CDC design for a concrete downstream index/reporting use case and document initial snapshot, resume position, duplicate behavior, and schema-change handling. Sources: S19 ch. 12 (read: CDC and stream processing); S38 ch. 14 [TOC].
- **P8.10 — Stateful stream processing and event time** — **Priority:** useful. Depends on: P8.0b, P8.5, P8.9. Learn event time versus processing time, windows, watermarks, late/out-of-order events, state, checkpoints, and recovery. Only implement a stream engine when the business workload needs continuous unbounded processing; a queue worker is not automatically a stream processor. **Proof:** a small event-time aggregation with a late-event policy and tests for delayed/out-of-order input; describe the recovery guarantee of the selected runtime. Sources: S19 ch. 12 (read); S75, Apache Flink 2.3.0, Timely Stream Processing.
- **P8.11 — Distributed batch and coordination patterns** — **Priority:** useful. Depends on: P8.0d, P8.5. Compare batch jobs with streams; learn partitioned work queues, map/reduce, scatter/gather, join/barrier, and work stealing at the level needed to choose a pattern. **Proof:** design a partitioned batch job with partial failure, retry, duplicate work, and aggregation behavior; state why a simpler worker queue is or is not sufficient. Sources: S19 ch. 11 (selected sections read); S29 ch. 8 and 11–13 (selected sections read); S28 ch. 14–15 [TOC].
